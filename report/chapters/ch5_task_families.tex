\chapter{Task Families and Algorithms}
\label{ch:task_families}

The system organizes all assistance into \textbf{two task families}:
\emph{Object Allocation}, which finds a requested object and guides the user all
the way to grasping it, and \emph{Navigation}, which guides the user to a
destination room through goal-directed exploration. Every capability the user can
invoke resolves into one of these two: hand-relative \emph{reach guidance} is the
terminal phase of an Object Allocation task, and the \emph{information queries}
(``describe'', ``repeat'') are auxiliary services built on the Object Allocation
perception stack. This chapter details both families as implemented. Throughout, a
common structure recurs: a perception stage that turns frames into \emph{trusted}
detections, a pure decision layer that turns detections into guidance, and a speech
policy that decides what actually reaches the user's ears. The perception stages
differ per family; the philosophy --- nothing unverified is ever spoken --- does
not.

\section{Object Allocation}
\label{sec:object_allocation}

Object Allocation answers ``find my cup''. When the session FSM enters
ObjectAllocationActive, an asynchronous loop starts, running at 5\,FPS over the
session's frame stream until the task completes, is cancelled, or times out. The
task progresses through two guidance regimes governed by a single tracker:
\emph{body-relative} guidance while the object is beyond reach (direction and
distance cues that move the user), and \emph{hand-relative} reach guidance once the
object is at arm's length and the user's hand is visible (cues that move the hand).
The hand-off between the two is automatic and reversible.

\subsection{Detection and Temporal Filtering}

Each frame passes through YOLOv8n with inference restricted to the requested COCO
class and a 0.35 confidence floor. Raw per-frame detections are too flickery to
speak: a \emph{temporal-consistency filter} over a sliding window of the last five
frames requires the target to be present in at least three of them before it is
trusted, killing single-frame false positives. When multiple instances are visible
(two chairs), guidance targets the most head-on one --- the box whose horizontal
centre is closest to the frame centre.

\subsection{Spatial Reasoning with Per-Class Size Priors}
\label{sec:spatial_reasoning_task}

The trusted box is classified into a horizontal region --- left / center / right,
split at 0.35 and 0.65 of frame width --- and a distance bucket. Distance uses the
larger of the box's width and height fractions (so both wide and tall objects are
handled), compared against a \emph{per-class near-at-arm's-reach threshold}: a
laptop at 40\% of frame width is near, a cup at 18\% is equally near, a phone at
12\% likewise --- same arithmetic, three correct answers, because the priors encode
real object sizes. The ratio of observed apparent size to the class threshold
determines the bucket: at or above 0.85 of the threshold the object is \emph{near};
at or below 0.35 it is \emph{far}; otherwise \emph{medium}. Classes without an
explicit prior fall back to a book-sized default. (The complete prior table is
given in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}, Table~\ref{tab:priors}.)

Bucket transitions are debounced --- two consecutive frames of the same
(region, distance) pair are required before the ``official'' bucket switches --- so
YOLO's box jitter at a threshold boundary cannot fire three contradictory phrases
in under a second. The debounce exists to filter jitter while the user is still,
not to hide real movement: when the client's motion monitor reports that the user
has started moving, the smoothed state is reset and new buckets are accepted
immediately, so guidance keeps up with actual position changes.

\subsection{Guidance Policy}

The tracker speaks on first sighting (``Found your cup, to your left''), on bucket
changes (``Your cup is straight ahead, a few steps away''), and re-affirms unchanged
guidance at most every 6\,s. Edge cases have explicit behaviors:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Never seen yet:} a periodic scan prompt every 8\,s --- ``I don't
        see your cup yet. Slowly turn around so I can look.'' The first prompt is
        delayed by a full interval so it does not talk over the ``Looking for your
        cup'' confirmation at task start.
  \item \textbf{Seen, then lost} for a full five-frame window: a one-shot loss
        announcement including the last known direction --- ``I lost sight of your
        cup. It was to your left.''
  \item \textbf{Never seen within 60\,s:} the task aborts with ``I couldn't find
        your cup. Stopping the search.''
\end{itemize}

Completion is user-driven --- the user says ``got it'' and the task ends with a
closing phrase naming the object --- with one exception, produced by the reach
phase described next.

\subsection{The Reach Guidance Phase}
\label{sec:reach_guidance}

When the target's smoothed distance bucket is \emph{near} and MediaPipe detects a
hand in frame, guidance switches from body-relative to \emph{hand-relative} cues.
MediaPipe is only invoked once the tracker classifies the target as near ---
running it earlier would waste roughly 15\,ms per frame while the target is still
across the room.

The controlling quantity is the offset between the index fingertip and the target
box centroid. Three states result:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \emph{approach} --- directional cues along the dominant axis: ``Move your
        hand to the right'', ``Move your hand up''. The dominant axis is chosen by
        \emph{pixel} magnitude rather than frame-fraction, because the frame is
        wider than it is tall and fractional comparison systematically biased cues
        toward the vertical axis.
  \item \emph{almost} --- fingertip within 15\% of the frame from the target
        centroid on both axes: ``Almost there. Reach forward,'' accompanied by a
        short haptic pulse on entry into this state.
  \item \emph{touching} --- fingertip inside the target's bounding box: ``Your hand
        is on the cup. Grasp it,'' with a long haptic pulse.
\end{itemize}

Touching is the single autonomous success exit in the whole system --- it fires
the \texttt{task\_complete} event without user confirmation, because the fingertip on the
object \emph{is} the confirmation, and it is emitted at most once per task. Reach
cues are throttled: an unchanged (state, direction) pair is re-spoken at most every
5\,s. A 3\,s grace period keeps the system silent (rather than reverting to
body-direction guidance) across momentary hand-detection dropouts, preventing the
two guidance regimes from alternating at 5\,FPS on hand-detection flicker; leaving
reach mode clears the throttle key so re-entering later is announced fresh.

\subsection{Information Queries}
\label{sec:info_queries}

Two voice queries, built directly on the Object Allocation perception stack, are
available at any time and never change task state. \textbf{``Describe''} (``what
do you see'', ``what's in front of me'') runs YOLOv8n over the current frame at a
raised 0.5 confidence floor --- only things the system is reasonably sure about are
worth mentioning to a user with no visual cross-check --- and reads back up to the
four highest-confidence detections with their regions, classified by the same
spatial-reasoning module the search loop uses: ``I see a laptop straight ahead, a
cup on your left, and a chair on your right.'' \textbf{``Repeat''} (``say that
again'') replays the last spoken guidance phrase, which the session records on
every utterance; the replay itself does not overwrite the record, so repeating
twice replays the same phrase.

\section{Navigation: Goal-Directed Exploration}
\label{sec:navigation}

Navigation is the deeper task family. The user names only a destination room; the
system explores toward it with no map, no markers, and no localization --- the
design bet is that \emph{semantic room recognition} plus \emph{door detection} plus
the user's own physical turning, tracked by compass, can substitute for the SLAM a
phone camera cannot do. The navigation loop runs at 3\,FPS --- its models are
heavier than Object Allocation's --- and the first navigation task in a server's
lifetime loads them with a spoken ``Give me a moment while I get ready'' rather
than going silent.

\subsection{Destination Model and Semantic Arrival}
\label{sec:goals_model}

Each supported destination maps to \emph{indicator objects}, split into primary
(near-unique to the room) and secondary (supporting, often shared across rooms), as
shown in Table~\ref{tab:goal_indicators}. Arrival is declared when at least one
primary or at least two secondary indicators are confirmed --- so a lone sink never
declares a kitchen (bathrooms have sinks), but a refrigerator does. All indicators
are COCO classes, so the stock detector produces them with no additional training.
Spoken destinations resolve through an alias table (``washroom'', ``restroom'',
``loo'' $\to$ bathroom; ``lounge'', ``den'' $\to$ living room; \ldots) with a
longest-alias substring fallback, so ``take me to the master bedroom'' still
resolves to \emph{bedroom}; a destination that resolves to nothing ends the task
with a spoken list of the rooms the system does know.

Indicator evidence is deliberately \emph{localized}: during a room scan, a class is
confirmed only when at least four sightings fall within one $90^\circ$ window (a
$30^\circ$ sector plus its two neighbours). A real fridge racks up its sightings in
one spot as the camera sweeps past; detector noise scatters around the room and can
never sum to an arrival.

\subsection{The Door Detector}
\label{sec:door_model}

COCO has no door class, so a dedicated detector was trained. Two public datasets
were merged and reduced to a single \texttt{door} class: DoorDetect
\cite{arduengo2021door} (386 door images plus 827 hard negatives --- handles and
cabinets --- that teach the model what a door is \emph{not}) and DeepDoors2
\cite{ramoa2021deepdoors} (3{,}000 images with 3{,}106 door boxes, converted from
segmentation masks by thresholding the mask colour and taking connected-component
bounding boxes), for a combined 4{,}213 images split 80/20 into 3{,}371 training
and 842 validation images. A YOLOv8n was fine-tuned from COCO-pretrained weights
(319 of 355 layers transferred) at $640$ input resolution, batch 8, AdamW; the best
checkpoint (epoch 39) achieves \textbf{mAP@50 $= 0.946$}, mAP@50--95 $= 0.829$,
precision $0.968$, and recall $0.892$ --- comfortably above the ${\geq}0.6$ mAP@50
target set for the milestone. A second, 4-class YOLOv8s model (door / handle /
cabinet door / refrigerator door) was trained on the original DoorDetect labels;
its recall is too low to detect doors alone, but that is not its job --- it serves
as a \emph{semantic verifier}, below. Both trained weight files are committed to
the repository so a fresh clone runs the complete system.

\subsection{The Three-Layer Door Perception Funnel}
\label{sec:door_funnel}

A false door is the most dangerous percept in the system --- it points a blind user
at a wall, a curtain, or a refrigerator. Raw detector confidence cannot prevent
this: in live testing, blank walls scored as doors at 0.9 confidence. Every door
candidate above the 0.45 confidence floor therefore descends a three-layer funnel
(Figure~\ref{fig:door_funnel}), each layer motivated by a specific observed failure.

Several subtleties deserve emphasis. First, edge density is \emph{not} a hard
reject. A plain door in dim light is as smooth as a wall (both around 0.01 edge
fraction), and confidence cannot break the tie (walls reach 0.9). Smooth candidates
are instead demoted to require semantic proof --- only the verifier seeing a door
or a handle there can pass them. This is what finally separated the lace curtain
(edge-rich, tall, door-sized, defeats all geometry) from real doors: curtains have
no handles. Second, the verifier's fridge veto applies only when it saw fridge
evidence \emph{without} door evidence at the same spot --- the verifier
hallucinates ``refrigerator door'' on real doors too, and vetoing frames it
simultaneously corroborated kept breaking detection streaks on the actual door.
Third, a vetoed door never becomes fridge evidence: the verifier also hallucinates
``refrigerator door'' on blank walls, and feeding those into arrival evidence
produced fake fridges; real fridges must be detected by the COCO model on its own.
Fourth, fridge arbitration is suspended during the directed indicator confirmation
phase, when the user is deliberately pointing the camera at an appliance and the
door model routinely fires on real fridges --- without the exemption, the
arbitration starved the confirmation of the very fridge sightings it was checking
for. Finally, where a surviving door box and a COCO object box still overlap
heavily (IoU $\geq 0.5$), only the higher-confidence claim is kept, eliminating
door/refrigerator double-claims.

The whole-frame gate is applied to COCO objects too: while the user stands
mid-room scanning or confirming, a ``refrigerator'' boxed edge-to-edge is a wall
latch and a fake kitchen indicator --- a direct path to a false arrival --- so
whole-scene object boxes are rejected in those phases. During the close door
approach, where filling the frame is legitimate, the gate is lifted.

\subsection{The Compass-Anchored Room Scan}
\label{sec:room_scan}

On entering a room (and at journey start), the system requests one slow full turn:
``Looking for the kitchen. Let's scan the room --- slowly turn to your right, all
the way around, until you are facing where you started.'' The goal echo is folded
into this instruction rather than spoken separately, so it cannot be cut off. The
first compass heading is locked as the anchor --- ``ahead'' means where the scan
began --- and every subsequent detection is filed by true bearing:
$\text{bearing} = \text{camera heading} + (x_{\text{frame}} - 0.5)\cdot
\text{FOV}$, the in-frame correction fixing a class of ``said left, was right''
errors. Sightings are accumulated into $12 \times 30^\circ$ sectors, and door and
indicator bearings are \emph{clustered} (sightings within $30^\circ$ merge,
including across the $\pm180^\circ$ seam) so one physical door is announced once,
with one precise heading. Indicator sightings record their class alongside the
bearing, so a later prompt can name what was seen (``I noticed a fridge on your
left''), not just point vaguely.

Scan completion requires \emph{both} ${\sim}350^\circ$ of accumulated rotation
\emph{and} a compass-confirmed return to within $25^\circ$ of the start heading. An
earlier design that ended on sector coverage alone could be faked by compass noise
mid-turn, ending the scan while the user faced the wrong way --- and every spoken
direction would then be computed from a broken premise. With the current rule, the
directions in the scan summary (``I found a door behind you, to the right'') are
true at the moment they are heard; if the rotation total is complete but the user
has not yet returned to the start heading, the system nudges (``Almost done ---
keep turning until you face where you started''). During the turn, exactly two
gentle progress nudges are spoken --- at $90^\circ$ and $270^\circ$ of rotation ---
with deliberately different wordings so ambient de-duplication does not swallow the
second.

A motion gate skips detection on blurred frames while the phone pans too fast
(frame-difference above a tuned threshold), coaching ``Slow down. Move the phone
slowly'' only after three consecutive fast frames --- a single blurred frame is an
autofocus hunt, not the user rushing --- while still advancing the rotation total so
a fast sweep cannot stall completion. Without a compass (a laptop, or permission
denied), the scan falls back to a single steady-pan pass over a fixed number of
usable frames, with no bearing summaries.

The controller (Figure~\ref{fig:nav_controller}) implements a two-phase principle:
Phase~1 scans silently and collects evidence; Phase~2 acts on that evidence by
priority. At scan end, the branches are evaluated strictly in order:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Strong indicator evidence} (the localized arrival bar was met)
        still receives a \emph{directed double-check}: ``I've seen signs of the
        kitchen on your right. Turn that way, and let's make sure we've reached
        it.'' The user is rotated toward the densest sighting cluster and the
        system re-confirms there before declaring arrival, so a scan artifact
        alone never ends a journey. (Without usable bearings --- no compass ---
        the confirmation is undirected: ``keep panning slowly.'')
  \item \textbf{Weak indicator sightings} (glimpsed, but below the arrival bar)
        get the same directed confirmation, naming the most-sighted class in the
        cluster: ``I noticed a fridge on your left. Let's double-check --- turn
        that way and point the camera at it.'' Indicator priority holds: this
        branch runs before doors are considered.
  \item \textbf{Doors:} a single confirmed door cluster becomes the target
        directly: the user is rotated to face it, then guided in. When several
        clusters survive the sighting minimum, the multi-door dialogue of
        Section~\ref{sec:multi_door} chooses among them --- by the user's spoken
        hint when one is given, by proximity straight ahead otherwise. Clusters
        below the sighting minimum are flickers, never targets. If the
        scan summary named exactly one finding --- this door --- the door is not
        announced twice.
  \item \textbf{Nothing useful:} the room is rescanned, with the announcement and
        the fresh scan instruction merged into a single utterance so instructions
        never collide.
\end{enumerate}

The directed confirmation (\texttt{go\_indicator}) uses a lower evidence bar than
the scan --- three sightings per class rather than four --- because it is a
re-check of evidence the scan already flagged, followed by a short settle window so
that neighbouring indicators (the fridge right next to the oven) cross the bar too
and the arrival line names \emph{all} of them: ``I can see a fridge and an oven ---
we've reached the kitchen.'' If confirmation fails within its window
(${\sim}10$\,s), the indicator had its chance: if the scan also flagged a door, the
system takes it (``I couldn't confirm the kitchen here. Let's take the door
instead''); otherwise a full rescan begins.

The guided turn (\texttt{face\_target}) computes the signed turn from the live
compass heading to the target's stored absolute heading. The scan-end announcement
already said which way to turn, so the turn itself is silent --- a re-instruction
in different words reads as two contradictory commands --- with a sparing nudge
(``Turn slowly to your right to face the door'') only if the user has still not
faced the target after several seconds. On facing it (within $25^\circ$), the
handoff is silent in both branches: the very next line is the one-shot door
call-out or the arrival itself, and a filler sentence would still be playing when
it arrives.

In \texttt{go\_door}, if the door cannot be re-confirmed for ${\sim}12$\,s of
observed frames, the flag is treated as stale and a full rescan begins (``I can't
find that door anymore\ldots''), with rotating ambient nudges (``Still looking for
a door. Keep moving the camera around the room.'') in the meantime.

\subsection{Door Approach: Distance in Steps}
\label{sec:door_distance}

Door distance uses a pinhole model over the door's known height: with doors
${\sim}2$\,m tall and the focal length derived from an assumed $60^\circ$ field of
view, $d = h_{\text{real}} \cdot f_{\text{px}} / h_{\text{px}}$. Two corrections
came from live testing: the focal length must be derived from the image's
\emph{long} side (a camera's quoted field of view belongs to its wider axis, and
phones stream portrait; deriving it from the width understated every distance by
${\sim}33\%$), and when the verifier also boxed the door, distance is measured on
the tighter (shorter) of the two boxes --- the primary model's boxes run loose at
range, and a fat box reads as near. The per-frame estimate is steadied by taking
the median over the temporal confirmation window, and a one-shot calibration
constant scales the whole chain from a single ground-truth measurement.

Distances are spoken in steps (${\sim}0.75$\,m each) with a tactile endgame: within
three steps, ``Walk about 2 steps, and reach out with your hand to find it'';
farther out, ``Walk forward, and after about $N$ steps reach out with your hand.''
The spoken sequence is strictly ordered: (1) one static call-out stating where the
door is --- ``The door is on your right, about 4 steps away. Let me check the path
ahead.'' --- phrased definitely (``The door is'', never ``There's a door'', which
sounds like a guess, because by this point the door has been confirmed); (2)
obstacle speech is held for ${\sim}2$\,s so that sentence finishes playing; (3) the
path verdict follows --- either ``The path is clear,'' plus the walking
instruction, or the obstacle warning. Safety information always precedes the
instruction to move.

\subsection{The Obstacle Watchdog}
\label{sec:obstacles}

Armed only in the walking phase (\texttt{go\_door}) --- the only phase the user
moves --- the watchdog fuses two layers with absolute speech priority over door
guidance.

\textbf{Layer A (named objects).} During the approach, the COCO detector
additionally requests a set of obstacle classes (person, chair, couch, bed, table,
plant, bags, bench, TV, dog, cat). Any box that sits low in frame (bottom below
62\% of frame height --- on the floor ahead, not hanging in the distance), is large
enough to matter (at least 18\% of frame height), and overlaps the central walking
lane (the 30--70\% horizontal band) by at least 10\% of frame width triggers a
warning naming the object: ``There's a chair in your path. Step to your right,
where it's clear,'' with the sidestep direction chosen away from the obstacle's
side of the lane. The minimum-overlap threshold exists because side furniture
merely grazing the lane edge used to trigger stop warnings.

\textbf{Layer B (unnamed clutter).} COCO has no word for the clutter that actually
litters floors --- clothes piles, boxes, bins, a standing fan. The question was
never ``is there an object?'' but ``is the strip of floor I am about to walk on
clear?'' --- so the class-agnostic signal is \emph{floor segmentation}: SegFormer
labels every pixel, and the lane just ahead of the user's feet (rows 66--94\% of
frame height, the central 38--62\% band) must remain at least 60\% walkable ---
floor, rug, or carpet pixels, plus \emph{door}, since in this phase the door is the
destination, never an obstacle. Something \emph{beside} the path leaves the floor
corridor continuous (silent); something \emph{in} the path punches a hole in it
(warn, as ``something'', with the sidestep direction toward the flank showing more
visible floor). This floor-based formulation replaced an earlier relative-depth
tripwire \cite{yang2024depthanything} that could not reliably separate beside-path
from in-path objects in cluttered rooms; the depth variant is retained in the code
as a configurable alternative, and if neither auxiliary model is available the
watchdog degrades gracefully to the named-object layer alone.

Both layers are debounced --- three consecutive blocked frames before the first
warning, two clear frames before release --- re-warn every ${\sim}3$\,s while
blocked (``It's still in your path --- step more to your right''), and close with
``Okay, the way ahead is clear,'' followed, in the same utterance, by
re-orientation toward the door if it is in sight (two back-to-back priority lines
would cut each other off). While an obstacle episode is open, a blocking frame
suppresses door guidance entirely. Once the user is at or through the doorway, the
floor signal is meaningless (the panel fills the frame) and pauses --- but a person
stepping into the doorway is still a named YOLO box, so the named layer stays armed
through the transit.

\subsection{Doorway Transit Detection}
\label{sec:transit}

Detecting ``the user just walked through'' has no single reliable signal, so the
system fuses several, each gated by the others. A tracked approach must first get
\emph{near} the door: either the median metric distance dropped below 2\,m, or the
confirmed door grew to fill at least 80\% of the frame height --- a scale-free
signal immune to calibration error. Then a saturated door-shaped box at arm's
length --- which the edge gate deliberately rejects as a confirmed door, since a
panel up close is smooth --- arms an ``at the door'' latch, but only after two
consecutive qualifying frames (a single loose box mid-sidestep once spoke ``you're
at the door'' from two meters away) and never while an obstacle episode is open
(the episode must close with its all-clear first, or the spoken order contradicts
itself). Arming the latch speaks the whole door sequence in one utterance ---
network latency between separate commands would leave the user waiting at the
door: ``You're right at the door. Reach out with your hand, open it, walk through
the doorway, and take two or three steps into the room.''

Transit is then inferred when the door disappears from view for ${\sim}2$\,s of
observed frames \emph{and} the camera reports sustained motion --- at least four
frames of frame-to-frame movement since reaching the door. The camera is the
odometer: walking produces sustained frame motion, standing still reads near zero,
and detection flicker without movement can never fake a walk-through; if the
visual thresholds fire but the user has not moved, the at-the-door instruction is
gently repeated instead. Far doors glimpsed mid-walk-through belong to the
\emph{next} room and deliberately do not reset the disappearance counter ---
letting them reset it once stalled the ``you're through'' announcement by
${\sim}16$\,s. Finally, a hold cap (${\sim}13$\,s) forces the inference if a new
room's own saturated door candidates keep the latch alive after the user has
clearly moved on. On transit, the new room's scan begins immediately, anchored to
the user's new heading, with the announcement and the scan instruction merged:
``You're through. Now slowly turn to your right, all the way around\ldots''

\subsection{Multi-Door Rooms}
\label{sec:multi_door}

When a scan finds several door clusters, the choice is conversational before it is
autonomous. The scan summary has already told the user how many doors there are and
where each one lies (``I found two doors: one ahead, and one on your left''), so
Lumen follows it with a question rather than a silent decision: if the user knows
which way the goal lies, they are invited to say so. A one-word answer ---
``left'', ``right'', ``ahead'', ``behind'' --- is matched against the doors'
bearings, the corresponding cluster becomes the target, and guidance proceeds
toward it. The user is treated as a \emph{direction oracle}: someone in their own
home often knows the way even when they cannot see it, and a spoken word is cheaper
than any amount of additional perception. If the user does not know --- they say so,
or simply give no hint --- the system decides for them, taking the door nearest
straight ahead: the choice that requires the least turning, keeps the camera's
evidence for it freshest, and is simplest to narrate. Either way the user is never
stranded on an unanswered question, every door was already named by direction so
the alternatives are known, and ``stop'' is honored at any moment. Remembering the
door just entered through --- so the same fork never has to be asked about twice ---
is a designed extension discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:future_work}.

\subsection{Speech Discipline}
\label{sec:speech_discipline}

A blind user cannot glance at a transcript; overlapping or stale audio is a
functional bug, not a cosmetic one. The navigation engine enforces a two-tier
policy. \emph{Priority} lines (scan summaries, phase changes, obstacle warnings,
the at-door sequence, arrival) are always spoken. \emph{Ambient} nudges (keep
scanning, turn corrections, door-hunt prompts) are dropped --- never queued --- if
an identical ambient line played within the last 8\,s or if \emph{any} line played
within the last 2.5\,s: the client plays clips back-to-back, and unthrottled
ambient chatter would queue up and lag behind reality. Announcements that would
otherwise be adjacent are merged into single utterances, because two back-to-back
priority lines would cut each other off; a flag carried across the phase change
suppresses the next phase's opening instruction when the previous announcement
already contained it. The scan-summary sentence exemplifies all three rules:
completion confirmation, findings by direction, and the next instruction arrive as
one connected utterance --- ``You're back where you started --- scan complete. I
found a door behind you, to the right. Turn toward it and point your camera at it,
so I can guide you in precisely.''
